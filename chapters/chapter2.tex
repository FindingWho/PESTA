%\section{Mechanics of Material Breakdown in Clinical Environments}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
While traditional acoustic stethoscopes remain the primary diagnostic tool in clinical practice, extensive literature demonstrates their susceptibility to mechanical degradation over time. Research into medical-grade polyvinyl chloride (PVC) polymers indicates that frequent exposure to standard clinical sanitizing agents, specifically isopropyl alcohol, accelerates the leaching of essential plasticizers from the polymer matrix \cite{josh2012temperature}. Furthermore, studies evaluating material fatigue in clinical environments establish that the continuous absorption of lipophilic fluids, such as human skin sebum, directly contributes to polymer hardening and surface micro-cracking \cite{zhang2024review}. While the chemical degradation of medical PVC is well-documented in polymer science, its direct correlation with acoustic transmission loss in diagnostic instruments remains underexplored.


%\section{Acoustic Principles and Diagnostic Risks of Stethoscope Degradation}

The structural integrity of the PVC tubing is the primary variable in preserving the acoustic pressure transmission of a stethoscope. Ertel et al. \cite{ertel1966stethoscope} established the foundational acoustics of stethoscope transmission, demonstrating that compromised structural integrity actively attenuates low-frequency biological signals. As the polymer hardens and loses its mechanical compliance, the tube's ability to reject external ambient noise is severely diminished. Leng et al. \cite{leng2015electronic} highlighted that this physical degradation in the acoustic pathway drastically lowers the signal-to-noise ratio, effectively masking faint physiological frequencies.

Despite these recognized diagnostic risks, there is a distinct lack of standardized, objective calibration frameworks designed to quantify this acoustic loss. While Klum et al. \cite{klum2019validation} proposed a validation system specifically for digital stethoscopes, a gap remains for a low-cost, automated calibration rig capable of continuously tracking the frequency response of traditional acoustic models across the 20-2000 Hz spectrum.

%\section{The Evolution of Auscultation: Acoustic vs. Electronic}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Recent advancements in medical diagnostics have driven a gradual transition from traditional acoustic stethoscopes to electronic variants. Electronic stethoscopes utilize digital amplification and active filtering to enhance clinical sensitivity. Studies by Fan et al. \cite{fan2022real} demonstrated that electronic tele-auscultation systems exhibit higher sensitivity in detecting subtle cardiovascular murmurs and pathological gallops compared to unamplified acoustic instruments. Despite these technological advantages, traditional acoustic stethoscopes remain the dominant clinical standard worldwide due to their durability, lack of power requirements, and significantly lower cost. Because the global healthcare infrastructure continues to rely heavily on these purely mechanical devices, establishing a standardized calibration rig to verify their acoustic fidelity remains a critical engineering priority.

%\section{Time-Frequency Analysis in Biomedical Acoustics}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
The accurate evaluation of cardiopulmonary acoustics requires specialized digital signal processing techniques, as biological sounds are inherently non-stationary signals. While standard Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithms are effective for extracting the overall frequency components of a signal, they completely discard temporal data, making it impossible to map exactly when a specific frequency occurred. According to Debbal et al. \cite{debbal2011computerized}, traditional FFT analysis is inadequate for distinguishing between transient cardiopulmonary events, such as the S1 and S2 heart sounds, which occur milliseconds apart. To resolve this, biomedical signal processing relies on the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), which computes the Fourier transform across overlapping sliding windows. The resulting spectrogram provides a two-dimensional time-frequency representation, allowing researchers to visualize both the frequency spectrum and the precise timing of acoustic events.
