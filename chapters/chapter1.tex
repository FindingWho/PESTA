\section{Problem Identification/Contextualization}

Cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases remain among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, requiring accurate, timely, and accessible diagnostic screening tools in clinical practice. The practice of clinical auscultation—listening to the internal biological sounds of the body—underwent a paradigm shift in 1816 with the invention of the first stethoscope by French physician René Laennec \cite{roguin2006rene}. Prior to this invention, physicians relied on immediate auscultation, placing their ear directly against the patient's chest, which posed significant acoustic and hygienic limitations. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:laennec_painting}, Laennec overcame this by channeling chest vibrations through a hollow cylinder, subsequently manufacturing the first formal monaural stethoscope from a turned wooden tube (Figure \ref{fig:first_stethoscope}) \cite{roguin2006rene}.

Over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the instrument evolved through the introduction of flexible binaural tubing by Arthur Leared and George Cammann in 1851, and was later refined by Rappaport, Sprague, and David Littmann into the modern dual-head chestpiece architecture \cite{weinberg1993color}. Today, traditional purely acoustic stethoscopes remain the dominant diagnostic instrument in global clinical practice due to their high mechanical durability, low acquisition cost, and complete independence from electrical power sources \cite{leng2015electronic, fan2022real}. 

However, despite two centuries of ergonomic refinement, the fundamental reliance on the passive mechanical transmission of acoustic pressure has remained unchanged. Consequently, purely acoustic auscultation is inherently constrained by two major factors: the subjective variability of human auditory perception and the unrecognized physical degradation of the instrument over time. Clinical studies have demonstrated that general practitioners miss up to 57\% of significant valvular heart diseases when relying on standard acoustic stethoscopes \cite{gardezi2017cardiac}. While this high diagnostic error rate is frequently attributed to differences in clinical training or human auditory thresholds, a substantial portion of this signal loss stems from unnoticed material and mechanical failures within the stethoscope itself.

An acoustic stethoscope operates as a hermetically sealed, passive mechanical wave-guide that captures structure-borne vibrations from the patient's chest wall and channels them as acoustic pressure waves to the clinician's ear canals \cite{leng2015electronic}. The fidelity of this acoustic transmission depends entirely on the physical integrity of its constituent materials: the chestpiece diaphragm and bell, the internal acoustic lumen, the flexible polyvinyl chloride (PVC) tubing, and the elastomeric eartips.

In routine hospital and clinic environments, stethoscopes are subjected to harsh chemical and mechanical stressors. Strict nosocomial infection control protocols require clinicians to sanitize their instruments between every patient encounter using isopropyl alcohol wipes or chlorine-based disinfectants. Repeated exposure to these chemical solvents actively leaches plasticizers from the PVC polymer matrix, while continuous contact with human skin lipids (sebum), caused by draping the instrument around the neck, catalyzes polymer stiffening and surface micro-cracking \cite{josh2012temperature, zhang2024review}. Simultaneously, diaphragms suffer from mechanical fatigue, and eartips lose their elastomeric compliance.

Because this material degradation occurs gradually over months and years of clinical service, it induces an insidious phenomenon: clinicians subconsciously adapt to the progressive loss of acoustic volume and spectral clarity, remaining entirely unaware that their instrument is compromised. When hardened tubing, micro-fissures, or compromised eartip seals alter the acoustic impedance of the pathway, critical low-frequency cardiovascular biomarkers, such as the pathological S3 and S4 ventricular gallops ($20\text{--}50\text{ Hz}$), which serve as primary indicators of congestive heart failure and ventricular hypertrophy, are severely attenuated before reaching the tympanic membrane \cite{ertel1966stethoscope}. Similarly, high-frequency pulmonary crackles and murmurs ($200\text{--}2000\text{ Hz}$) become masked by ambient room noise penetrating the rigid tubing walls \cite{leng2015electronic}.

This silent mechanical attenuation directly results in false-negative diagnoses, delayed medical interventions, and compromised patient safety. Furthermore, unlike electronic medical equipment, such as electrocardiographs, defibrillators, or infusion pumps, which undergo mandatory periodic metrological verification in hospital clinical engineering departments, acoustic stethoscopes are rarely, if ever, subjected to quantitative performance testing. Establishing an objective, repeatable, and low-cost electroacoustic calibration framework is therefore not merely a matter of instrument maintenance, but a vital clinical requirement to eliminate diagnostic blind spots and ensure patient safety.

\section{Research Questions}

Following the problem identification and the PICO formulation, a central research question was established to guide the engineering development of this project: \textit{Is it possible to design and validate a low-cost, microcontroller-based electroacoustic testing rig capable of objectively characterizing the frequency response and signal fidelity of stethoscopes across the full $20\text{--}2000\text{ Hz}$ cardiopulmonary bandwidth?}

To systematically address this question, four specific research questions (RQ) were defined:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} What are the primary physical and chemical degradation mechanisms in modern stethoscopes, and how do they acoustically alter the transmission of critical cardiopulmonary biomarkers across low ($20\text{--}50\text{ Hz}$), intermediate ($200\text{ Hz}$), and high ($1000\text{--}2000\text{ Hz}$) frequency bands?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How can a low-cost Class-AB power amplifier and mechanical surface exciter be engineered to generate structure-borne acoustic signals that accurately replicate the frequency spectrum of human cardiopulmonary sounds, while maintaining linearity and minimizing Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) across the $20\text{--}2000\text{ Hz}$ diagnostic bandwidth?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent do analog signal conditioning constraints introduce amplitude instabilities or Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) into the calibration baseline?
\end{itemize}

\section{Objectives and Motivation}

\subsection*{General Objective}
To design, implement, and validate an integrated, electroacoustic calibration and spectral visualization system suitable for the objective characterization of stethoscope frequency response and signal fidelity across the $20\text{--}2000\text{ Hz}$ cardiopulmonary bandwidth.

\subsection*{Specific Objectives}
Currently, the assessment of stethoscope integrity in clinical practice relies on subjective, ad-hoc auditory checks by healthcare professionals, allowing degraded instruments to remain in active rotation. To complement clinical practice with objective and verifiable engineering metrics, this work establishes the following specific objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Investigate Stethoscope Degradation and Biomedical Acoustics:} Conduct a comprehensive literature review on stethoscope mechanical architecture, material degradation mechanisms, medical device metrology, and the acoustic signatures of physiological and pathological cardiopulmonary sounds.
    \item \textbf{Simulate Structure-Borne Cardiopulmonary Acoustics:} Study and implement an electroacoustic excitation stage and tissue-simulating coupling medium capable of generating controlled vibrations that mimic the frequency spectrum and acoustic impedance of the human thorax.
    \item \textbf{Capture High-Fidelity Acoustic Data:} Evaluate and integrate an acoustic sensing, pre-amplification, and analog filtering topology capable of coupling to a stethoscope eartip and capturing faint low-frequency signals without introducing significant distortion or environmental noise.
    \item \textbf{Develop a High-Speed Digitization and Spectral Analysis Pipeline:} Identify and implement a digital acquisition and signal processing methodology capable of satisfying the Nyquist sampling criterion across the target medical bandwidth and computing time-frequency representations of the acquired audio.
    \item \textbf{Establish a Repeatable Validation Protocol:} Formulate a standardized testing protocol using controlled sinusoidal frequency sweeps to characterize system linearity, bandwidth limits, and harmonic distortion in a repeatable manner.
\end{itemize}

\section{Report Organization}

This report is organized into six main chapters, structured as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter 1 -- Introduction:} Contextualizes the clinical problem of stethoscope degradation, justifies the metrological importance of the work, defines the PICO framework, research questions, general and specific objectives, project schedule, and overall document structure.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 2 -- Literature Review:} Presents the theoretical and scientific foundation of the project.     \item \textbf{Chapter 3 -- Methodology:} Describes the engineering methodology, functional and non-functional requirements, electromechanical system architecture, hardware component selection, circuit schematics, mathematical filter calculations, microcontroller digitization parameters, and the Python DSP spectral analysis pipeline.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 4 -- Results and Discussion:} Presents the experimental characterization of the prototype across controlled sinusoidal frequency sweeps ($20\text{ Hz}$, $50\text{ Hz}$, $200\text{ Hz}$, and $1111\text{ Hz}$). It analyzes time-domain amplitude envelopes and STFT spectrograms, evaluates Automatic Gain Control (AGC) artifacts and Total Harmonic Distortion (THD), and discusses the broader clinical and economic implications of stethoscope calibration versus electronic replacement.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 5 -- Conclusion:} Synthesizes the main engineering contributions of the work, directly answers the proposed research questions, outlines the technical limitations encountered in the prototype, and establishes concrete directions for future development.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 6 -- Appendices:} Contains the complete, commented source code listings developed for the ESP32 and the Python serial logging and spectral visualization algorithms.
\end{itemize}
